# Schimel rules

Distilled from Joshua Schimel, *Writing Science* (OUP, 2012). Anchors (`§`) point to
that book so the author can read the principle in full. The agent has no access to the
book and works from this file alone.

**How to use this file.** Every entry is a test. Run each against the manuscript. A
failure is reported as an `[[ ]]` annotation carrying the anchor, the quoted target,
and a direction. Do not extend an entry past what it states. Every entry carries one
of two markers. `†APPLY` — the agent acts without asking, because the correct result
does not depend on taste, and only within the current phase's role boundary. Where the
phase cannot act, the agent reports the failure instead. `†JUDGMENT` — the entry is the
author's to decide: name the finding, carry the anchor, and stop. The same applies to
any case an entry under-determines, whatever its marker. The author reads that section.

**Vocabulary.**

- **OCAR** — Opening, Challenge, Action, Resolution. The elements of a story arc.
- **O / C-A / R** — at the sentence scale: the opening names the topic, the
  challenge-action carries the verb and the event, the resolution delivers the outcome.
  Schimel maps these onto subject, verb, and object (§12).
- **TS-D / LD / LDR / OCAR** — paragraph types, a spectrum from point-first to
  point-last (§11). *TS-D*: topic sentence, then development. *LD*: a lead of one or
  more sentences that states the point, then development. *LDR*: lead, development,
  then a closing synthesis. *OCAR*: opens by setting the stage, resolves at the end.
- **Arc** — a unit with an opening and a resolution. Arcs nest: a paper, a section, a
  paragraph, a sentence (§10).

**Three exclusions, so they are not re-added later.**

- Schimel offers four story structures (OCAR, ABDCE, LD, LDR) selected by audience
  patience (§4.1). Only OCAR is used here: these phases target peer-reviewed journal
  articles, which are OCAR-shaped.
- Schimel's advice on word choice by etymology — preferring short Anglo-Saxon words
  over long Latin ones (§15.3) — is excluded. It is a register change, and these phases
  preserve the author's voice.
- SUCCES, the six factors of stickiness that frame the rest of the book (§3), is
  excluded. No test here derives from it.

## Paper and section

- **The paper has all four OCAR elements.** Name the challenge, or report it absent.
  †APPLY (§4)
- **The opening sets the scene before it makes an argument.** Name the system, the
  organisms, the problem, and the audience. †JUDGMENT (§5)
- **The opening fails in two ways**: it sends the reader somewhere the paper does not
  go, or it gives no direction at all. †JUDGMENT (§5.2)
- **The opening narrows.** It moves from the general to the specific, closing on the
  knowledge gap and then the specific question. †JUDGMENT (§6)
- **The opening is wide and the resolution is equally wide.** The paper narrows to the
  challenge and widens again to the significance. †JUDGMENT (§4)
- **The challenge appears late in a straight OCAR paper.** †APPLY (§4.1)
- **The resolution states what was learned and what it now means.** †JUDGMENT (§9)
- **Every section and subsection is a complete arc.** It opens by setting up its topic
  and closes by resolving it. †JUDGMENT (§10)
- **No idea is discussed in two places.** An idea split across sections has a broken
  arc. †APPLY (§10)
- **This is the story of the research, not a chronology of the data.** A sequential
  design, in which each experiment builds on the last, is told chronologically.
  †JUDGMENT (§8)

## Paragraph

- **Each paragraph carries exactly one point.** Identify it and quote the sentence that
  states it. †JUDGMENT (§11.2)
- **Point-first is the default and should dominate.** †JUDGMENT (§11.1)
- **Point-last paragraphs are legitimate but belong at openings, resolutions, and
  transitions.** Expect 25–30 percent of them. †JUDGMENT (§11.2)
- **The type is a spectrum, not a category.** A paragraph may sit anywhere between
  point-first and point-last, and may be hard to classify. Report the dominant position
  of the point, not a hard label. †JUDGMENT (§11.2)
- **Long paragraphs need a resolution.** A multi-sentence lead is fine, but the
  paragraph must close on its point. †JUDGMENT (§11.2)
- **A paragraph that rambles should be split.** †APPLY (§11.3)
- **Adjacent paragraphs connect.** The next paragraph's opening topic derives from the
  previous paragraph's resolution. †JUDGMENT (§13.1)

## Sentence

- **The sentence's topic is something the reader already holds.** Whatever opens the
  sentence is read as its topic; leading with new information implies it is old.
  †JUDGMENT (§12.1)
- **The key message sits at the stress** — the end of the main clause. †APPLY (§12.2)
- **Weighting runs stress, then topic, then middle.** Put what matters most at the end
  and let the middle carry detail. †JUDGMENT (§12.3)
- **The verb follows its subject closely.** A parenthetical or aside between them makes
  the sentence hard to follow. †APPLY (§12.4)
- **The topic is not buried under a qualifying clause.** †JUDGMENT (§12.5.1)
- **The stress is not buried under trailing words.** Words dangling after the real
  message should be cut or moved into the middle. †APPLY (§12.5.2)
- **A long sentence is judged by structure, not by length.** A long sentence works when
  it is right-opening: the point in a short first clause, nuance in subordinates. †APPLY
  (§12.6)
- **Adjacent sentences link stress to topic.** The next sentence's topic derives from
  the previous sentence's stress. †JUDGMENT (§13)
- **Short lists are acceptable; sustained listing is not.** †JUDGMENT (§13)
- **When flow breaks, check upstream first.** A reach-back fix can produce local flow
  and global disruption. †JUDGMENT (§13.1)
- **Passive voice is not a defect.** Ask whether the actor is deliberately withheld and
  whether the sentence is about the right thing. †JUDGMENT (§14.1)
- **Fuzzy verbs report that something happened without saying what.** Name the action.
  †JUDGMENT (§14.2)
- **Nominalizations hide the action.** A verb turned into a noun forces a weak verb to
  take its place. †JUDGMENT (§14.3)

## Word

- **Spell out every abbreviation or acronym on first occurrence**, except terms common
  enough that every reader knows them. †APPLY (§15.1.2)
- **A new technical term placed in the topic position reads as jargon.** Placing it at
  the stress reads as a definition; the middle de-emphasizes it. †JUDGMENT (§15.1.1)
- **A term is jargon when it names something the reader lacks and a plain equivalent
  exists.** The boundary depends on the audience and is not the agent's to decide. *A
  chemist knows "mole"; a public audience does not.* †JUDGMENT (§15.1)

## Condensing

- **Do not delete the words that build flow and coherence.** †JUDGMENT (§16.10)
- **A first draft may be verbose.** With coauthors, extra words are cheap to strip and
  expensive to add back. The deletions below apply to revision, not to drafting.
  †JUDGMENT (§16.10)
- **Each word should add content, clarify meaning, or provide coherence.** This is the
  test for every deletion. †APPLY (§16.1)
- **Delete redundancies** — the same fact stated twice. †APPLY (§16.2)
- **Delete statements that are merely obvious** to the audience. †APPLY (§16.3)
- **Delete modifiers that add nothing** — adverbs and adjectives carrying no
  information. Some clarify or define their referent and must be kept. †APPLY (§16.4)
- **Delete meta-discourse** that is obvious or redundant. Some is necessary to develop
  the flow of an argument and is kept. †APPLY (§16.5)
- **Delete verbosity**, and prefer the plainer equivalent where one exists. †APPLY
  (§16.6)
