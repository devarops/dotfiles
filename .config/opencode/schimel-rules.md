# Schimel rules

Distilled from Joshua Schimel, *Writing Science* (OUP, 2012). Anchors (`§`) point to
that book so the author can read the principle in full. The agent has no access to the
book and works from this file alone.

**How to use this file.** Every entry is a test. Run each against the manuscript. A
failure is reported as an `[[ ]]` annotation carrying the anchor, the quoted target,
and a direction. Do not extend an entry past what it states. Where an entry says the
judgment is the author's, the agent names the finding and stops.

**Vocabulary.**

- **OCAR** — Opening, Challenge, Action, Resolution. The elements of a story arc.
- **O / C-A / R** — at the sentence scale: the opening names the topic, the
  challenge-action carries the verb and the event, the resolution delivers the outcome.
  Schimel maps these onto subject, verb, and object (§12).
- **TS-D / LD / LDR / OCAR** — paragraph types, a spectrum from point-first to
  point-last (§11). *TS-D*: topic sentence, then development. *LD*: a lead of one or
  more sentences that states the point, then development. *LDR*: lead, development,
  then a closing synthesis. *OCAR*: opens by setting the stage, resolves at the end.
- **Arc** — a unit with an opening and a resolution. Arcs nest: a paper, a section, a
  paragraph, a sentence (§10).

**Two exclusions, so they are not re-added later.**

- Schimel offers four story structures (OCAR, ABDCE, LD, spiral) selected by audience
  patience (§4.1). Only OCAR is used here: these phases target peer-reviewed journal
  articles and theses, which are OCAR-shaped.
- Schimel's advice on word choice by etymology — preferring short Anglo-Saxon words
  over long Latin ones (§15.3) — is excluded. It is a register change, and these phases
  preserve the author's voice.

## Paper and section

- **The paper has all four OCAR elements.** Name the challenge, or report it absent.
  *A story missing an element is unsatisfying and unslippery rather than sticky (§4).*
- **The opening sets the scene before it makes an argument.** Name the system, the
  organisms, the problem. *Readers need their bearings before they can follow a claim (§5).*
- **The opening narrows.** It moves from the general to the specific, closing on the
  knowledge gap and then the specific question. *A funnel that never narrows leaves the
  reader without a problem to solve (§6).*
- **The opening is wide and the resolution is equally wide.** The paper narrows to the
  challenge and widens again to the significance. *Both ends of the hourglass must be
  the same width, or the ending feels like a summary rather than a payoff (§4, §5).*
- **The challenge appears late in a straight OCAR paper.** *OCAR develops slowly and
  suits a patient, specialist audience (§4.1).*
- **The resolution states what was learned and what it now means.** *A resolution that
  only summarizes the action has not closed the circle (§9).*
- **Every section and subsection is a complete arc.** It opens by setting up its topic
  and closes by resolving it. *Beginnings and endings are power positions; without them
  the reader cannot tell what mattered (§10).*
- **No idea is discussed in two places.** An idea split across sections has a broken
  arc. *Fragmented arcs force the reader to hold two threads and repeat themselves (§10).*
- **This is the story of the research, not a chronology of the data.** *Readers
  assemble the story themselves from data laid out in the order it was collected
  (§2, §3).*

## Paragraph

- **Each paragraph carries exactly one point.** Identify it and quote the sentence that
  states it. *A paragraph that cannot name its point is a point-nowhere paragraph, and
  the reader loses the thread (§11.2).*
- **Point-first is the default and should dominate.** Topic-first paragraphs let a
  reader skim the first sentence of each and still get the paper. *TS-D is the
  bread-and-butter form (§11.1).*
- **Point-last paragraphs are legitimate but belong at openings, resolutions, and
  transitions.** Expect roughly a quarter of them. *They are a deliberate choice for
  load-bearing positions, not a variation to sprinkle through the body (§11.2).*
- **The type is a spectrum, not a category.** A paragraph may sit anywhere between
  point-first and point-last, and may be hard to classify. *Report the dominant
  position of the point, not a hard label (§11.2).*
- **Long paragraphs need a resolution.** A multi-sentence lead is fine, but the
  paragraph must close on its point. *Long paragraphs lean toward LDR or OCAR precisely
  so the point is not lost (§11.2).*
- **A paragraph that rambles should be split.** *An over-long, incoherent paragraph is
  usually several threads that each need their own arc (§11.3).*
- **Adjacent paragraphs connect.** The next paragraph's opening topic should derive from
  the previous paragraph's resolution. *Paragraph breaks signal a new arc, and readers
  expect the new arc to build on the old one (§13.1).*

## Sentence

- **The sentence's topic is something the reader already holds.** Whatever opens the
  sentence is read as its topic; leading with new information implies it is old.
  *Readers have no framework to hang unfamiliar information on, and will skim it (§12.1).*
- **The key message sits at the stress** — the end of the main clause. *Last words carry
  the greatest weight in English (§12.2).*
- **Weighting runs stress, then topic, then middle.** *This is Clark's 2-3-1 rule; put
  what matters most at the end and let the middle carry detail (§12.3).*
- **The verb follows its subject closely.** A parenthetical or aside between them makes
  the sentence hard to follow. *The longer the gap between actor and action, the duller
  the sentence (§12.4).*
- **The topic is not buried under a qualifying clause.** *An incidental qualifier at the
  front buries the subject and gets skimmed (§12.5.1).*
- **The stress is not buried under trailing words.** Words dangling after the real
  message should be cut or moved into the middle. *A qualifier that ends the sentence
  becomes the point of the sentence (§12.5.2).*
- **A long sentence is judged by structure, not by length.** A long sentence works when
  it is right-opening: the point in a short first clause, nuance in subordinates. *A
  long sentence that makes the reader wait for the point fails (§12.6).*
- **Adjacent sentences link stress to topic.** The next sentence's topic should derive
  from the previous sentence's stress. *Topic-to-topic makes a list; stress-to-topic
  makes a story (§13).*
- **Short lists are acceptable; sustained listing is not.** *A short list of related
  facts reads as coherent. A whole paragraph of them reads as disconnected (§13).*
- **When flow breaks, check upstream first.** A reach-back fix can produce local flow and
  global disruption. *Revising is top-down; the sentence that looks broken may be
  downstream of one that points the wrong way (§13.1).*
- **Passive voice is not a defect.** Ask whether the actor is deliberately withheld and
  whether the sentence is about the right thing. *Schimel keeps the passive where the
  actor is genuinely unimportant or deliberately unnamed (§14.1).*
- **Fuzzy verbs report that something happened without saying what.** Name the action.
  *A verb that masks the event leaves the reader to guess what occurred (§14.2).*
- **Nominalizations drain action.** A verb turned into a noun removes the actor and the
  event. *Prefer the verb; nominalization is the commonest way scientific prose goes
  dead (§14.3).*

## Word

- **Spell out every acronym on first occurrence**, except terms common enough that
  everyone knows them. *An undefined abbreviation assumes every possible reader already
  knows the term, and excludes the readers who don't (§15.1.2).*
- **A new technical term placed in the topic position reads as jargon.** Placing it at
  the stress reads as a definition; the middle de-emphasizes it. *Position is what
  controls whether a term feels known, defined, or incidental (§15.1.1).*
- **A term is jargon when it names something the reader lacks and a plain equivalent
  exists.** The boundary depends on the audience and is not the agent's to decide.
  *A chemist knows "mole"; a public audience does not. The same word is jargon in one
  room and terminology in another (§15.1).*
- **Do not delete the words that build flow and coherence.** *Condensing and clarity
  are the goals, but a transition the reader needs is not slack — it does real work
  (§16.10).*
- **Each word should add content, clarify meaning, or provide coherence.** *This is the
  test for every deletion (§16.1).*
- **Delete redundancies** — the same fact stated twice. *Repeats waste space and
  signal the reader to skim (§16.2).*
- **Delete statements that are merely obvious** to the audience. *Obvious content still
  costs the reader a fixation (§16.3).*
- **Delete modifiers that add nothing** — adverbs and adjectives carrying no
  information. *Many are empty amplifiers that inflate without specifying (§16.4).*
- **Delete meta-discourse** — statements about the document rather than the science.
  *Text about the text competes with the science for the reader's attention (§16.5).*
- **Delete verbosity**, and prefer the plainer equivalent where one exists. *A common
  word engages a stronger schema than an elaborate one (§15.2, §16.6).*
